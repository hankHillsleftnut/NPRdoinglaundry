# Psychology instruments — what we can actually use

Research pass, 17 October 2026. Asked for: what does psychology say about the
questions you need to ask to understand who someone is — personality, behaviour,
trauma.

**Everything here was searched and read, not recalled.** Where I am relying on a
secondary description rather than the primary instrument, it says so. This
matters because I have fabricated a citation in this project before.

**Editable by design.** Every candidate is a row you can cut, in the tables at the
bottom.

---

## The core finding, and it reshapes the approach

Psychology has two kinds of instrument, and only one of them fits this product.

**Instruments that ask.** Questionnaires. The Aspirations Index, the Loyola
Generativity Scale, personal strivings. They need the person to sit down and
answer, usually on a scale.

**Instruments that code.** Systems for scoring text someone has already produced.
Redemption and contamination sequences, agency and communion, narrative coherence.
They need no cooperation at all — only words.

**For an ambient product, coding systems are worth far more than questionnaires**,
and they are the part of this literature nobody building in this space seems to
use. You already have the words. You do not want to interrogate anyone. The
coding tradition was built for precisely that situation — researchers scoring
transcripts of people who had already stopped talking.

This also answers the question underneath yours. You asked what questions we need
to ask. The better answer is: **mostly none. We need to know what to look for in
what they already said.**

## The instruments, assessed

### 1. The Life Story Interview II — McAdams, 2007

**What it is.** The structured protocol behind narrative identity research. The
Study of Lives Research Group at Northwestern — successor to the Foley Center,
which ran 1997–2019 — calls the LSI-II
"[the best overall generic protocol for doing life story research](https://sites.northwestern.edu/studyoflivesresearchgroup/instruments/)."

**Its structure**, per the group's own instruments page and secondary accounts:

- **Life chapters.** The person is asked to think of their life as a book with
  chapters, and to describe them.
- **Key scenes**, each probed the same way: a **high point**, a **low point**, a
  **turning point**. For each: what happened, who was involved, what they were
  thinking and feeling, and why the scene matters to them.
- **Characters** — the people who played hero and villain roles.
- Runs about two hours, recorded and transcribed.

**Verdict: adopt the question shape, not the interview.** Nobody is sitting for two
hours at onboarding. But the four probes on every scene — *what happened, who was
there, what were you thinking, why does it matter* — are a near-perfect template
for the extractor, and the three scene types are a better organising principle for
a life than a date range.

**Caveat on my own sourcing:** I could not extract the verbatim prompts. The
Northwestern PDF uses subset fonts and would not decode. The structure above is
from the group's instruments page and secondary descriptions, which agreed with
each other. Exact wording needs the PDF read by a human.

### 2. Redemption and contamination coding — 1999 and 1998

**What it is.** Two coding systems for narrative accounts, listed among the same
group's instruments. A **redemption** sequence moves from bad to good; a
**contamination** sequence moves from good to bad. They are applied to text, not
asked.

**Why this is the most interesting item in the whole survey.** It is a measurable
property of *how someone narrates their own life*, it requires nothing from them,
and it bears directly on well-being. A 2018 study of 121 adults
([Alea, 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0276236617733837))
found redemption appeared spontaneously in 75% of turning points and 50% of low
points.

**Verdict: adopt.** This is a legitimate, non-clinical, text-derived read on how
someone processes difficulty — which is close to what you were reaching for with
"how they behave and all their trauma," without going anywhere near diagnosis.

A person whose low points consistently contaminate rather than redeem is telling
you something real about how they are doing. And the finding that redemption is
*usually* spontaneous means its absence is the signal.

### 3. Agency and communion coding — 2002, revised by Adler

**What it is.** Scores a narrative on two dimensions. Agency runs from
**powerlessness to mastery**; communion runs from **loneliness to warmth and
intimacy**. The revised system rates quality rather than counting mentions. It
descends from older TAT systems for power/achievement and intimacy/affiliation
motives.

**Verdict: adopt.** Two numbers, derived from text, that map almost exactly onto
things the product wants to know — does this person feel in control of their life,
and do they feel connected. Both are canonical-question material (questions 27, 30,
23) and neither requires asking.

---

### 4. The Aspirations Index — Kasser & Ryan, 1993

**What it is.** Seven categories of life goal, five items each. Three
**extrinsic** — wealth, fame, image. Three **intrinsic** — meaningful
relationships, personal growth, community contribution. Health does not sit
cleanly in either.

Each is rated three ways: **how important it is, how likely they think they are to
attain it, and how much they have already attained.**

**The findings.** Strong extrinsic aspirations are associated with worse mental
health; valuing intrinsic goals, with better. And longitudinally
([Sheldon & Kasser, 1998](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/2001_SheldonKasser_DevPsych.pdf)),
*attaining* intrinsic goals improved well-being while attaining extrinsic goals
did little.

**Verdict: adopt the three-way rating, skip the questionnaire.** That triple —
importance, expected attainment, actual attainment — **is the stated-versus-real
gap the product is built around**, already formalised and validated. Goal 8 wants
exactly this structure.

And the intrinsic/extrinsic split gives goal 21 something it badly needs: a
*principled* basis for a hypothesis about what someone really wants, rather than a
vibe. "You describe growth as what matters and spend your time on image" is a
framework-grounded observation, not a guess.

The official instrument and scoring are
[hosted by SDT](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/aspirations-index).

---

### 5. Personal strivings — Emmons, and Personal Projects Analysis — Little

**What they are.** Both **idiographic**: the person generates their own goals
rather than picking from a list, then rates each one. Emmons' Striving Assessment
Scales use 17 dimensions in the 1999 version, including affective tone,
probability of success, clarity, effort and perceived progress. Little's version
rates importance, difficulty and commitment. Emmons also used a semi-projective
sentence-completion format rather than direct questions.

**The finding that matters most.** Emmons studied **conflict among strivings** and
its consequences for well-being — goals that undermine each other.

**Verdict: adopt the conflict idea specifically.** Goal 12 asks what is impeding
progress, and the answer is often not a behaviour but *another goal*. "You want to
ship this and you want to be present at home, and those are fighting" is a better
impediment than "you skipped three Thursdays," and this literature says so.

The 17 rating dimensions are mostly over-specified for us. **Clarity, perceived
progress and effort** are worth keeping; they are things we can estimate from
behaviour rather than ask.

**Caveat:** my sources here were secondary — papers describing both methods, not
the instruments themselves. Most are 1990s–2000s and I found no current
psychometric review.

---

### 6. Loyola Generativity Scale · Northwestern Ego Integrity Scale

Twenty-item self-report on generativity — concern for promoting future
generations' well-being. The NEIS covers Erikson's eighth stage, integrity versus
despair, and the group's own page notes it "has received limited research
attention so far."

**Verdict: skip both for now.** Generativity is a real and interesting dimension,
but it is a 20-item questionnaire measuring something slow-moving, and nothing in
the product's current goals depends on it.

## Trauma — asked for, and the honest answer is no

You asked for trauma to be in scope. The literature's own position makes that a
clear no for the instrument you would reach for, and the sources are unusually
direct.

**The ACE questionnaire should not be self-administered.**

- **Its own creator says it was not built for this.** Robert Anda has said the
  questionnaire was designed to research, not screen, the relationship between
  childhood adversity and later outcomes.
- **It cannot diagnose.** [NACCHO guidance](https://www.naccho.org/uploads/downloadable-resources/ACEs-Questionnaire-Use_final.pdf)
  states plainly that it cannot be used to diagnose a mental or physical health
  condition, and that it is not predictive at the individual level.
- **Self-administration is named as a harm.** NACCHO: encouraging people to
  complete the questionnaire on their own time "does not align with the
  questionnaire's intended use and has the potential to cause harm." It lists
  risks of being distressing, retraumatising and stigmatising.
- **The score misestimates individuals by construction.** The
  [American Journal of Preventive Medicine](https://www.ajpmonline.org/article/S0749-3797(20)30058-1/fulltext)
  review notes the algorithm inherently leads to both over- and underestimation of
  individual risk.
- Finkelhor argues universal screening is premature without a clear clinical
  response available — which a phone app definitionally does not have.

**So: the product never scores trauma, and goal 21's non-clinical rule stands.**
This is not caution on my part; it is the instrument's authors and the public
health bodies that publish it saying so.

**What to do instead, which gets at the same thing responsibly.** Contamination
sequences (§2) and low agency (§3) are validated, text-derived, non-diagnostic
reads on exactly the territory you were pointing at — someone for whom difficult
things stay difficult, who narrates themselves as without power. The product can
notice that pattern, speak to it gently, and never name a cause.

Noticing that someone's hard chapters do not resolve is observation.
Naming why is diagnosis. The line is clean and this literature draws it in the
same place.

## What is unverified, and what it would take

1. **LSI-II verbatim wording.** The PDF would not decode. Needs a human to open
   it.
2. **Whether the coding systems work when applied by a model.** They were designed
   for trained human raters with inter-rater reliability checks. Whether an LLM
   reproduces those judgements on journal text is **an open empirical question and
   the single most important thing to test** — three of my strongest
   recommendations depend on it. This is a validation-gate item, not an assumption.
3. **Current psychometrics for strivings and personal projects.** My sources were
   secondary and mostly twenty years old.
4. **Nothing here was tested against your data.** No instrument in this document
   has run on a single Retrospect row.
